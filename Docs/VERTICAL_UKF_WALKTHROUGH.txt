A. WHY ALTITUDE ESTIMATION NEEDS FILTERING
-------------------------------------------
A paraglider pilot cares about two numbers: how high am I, and how fast am I
going up or down. No single sensor gives both well.

  Barometer (BMP585 x 2)  measures pressure -> altitude. No drift over
                          seconds, but noisy: on a worn device, airflow and
                          body movement add tens of centimetres of jitter,
                          and weather slowly shifts it. Differentiating a
                          noisy altitude to get speed amplifies the noise.
  Accelerometer (via the attitude estimator)
                          measures vertical acceleration 100 times a second
                          with little delay. But it says nothing about
                          altitude or speed directly. Integrating it twice
                          turns any small bias into a growing error: a bias
                          of 0.1 m/s^2 becomes 5 m of altitude error after
                          10 s.
  GNSS (NEO-M9N)          gives vertical velocity at a few Hz, with its own
                          noise and delay, and only when the sky is visible.

Filtering means combining them so that each covers the others' weakness:
the accelerometer gives quick response, the barometer stops the drift, and
GNSS (when present) anchors the velocity. The filter also has to say how
sure it is, because a safety system must not act on a number it cannot
trust.


B. WHAT A "STATE" IS
---------------------
The state is the smallest set of numbers that describes "where things are
now" well enough to predict the near future. Ours is

    x = [ h , vz , az ]
          |    |    +-- vertical acceleration, m/s^2, positive UP, gravity removed
          |    +------- vertical velocity, m/s, positive UP
          +------------ altitude, m, RELATIVE to where the barometers were calibrated

In code this is pg::VerticalStateVec, a nav::Vec<3> (three floats). The
index names live in NavigationTypes.hpp:

    namespace vstate {
    constexpr unsigned kAltitude = 0u;
    constexpr unsigned kVelocity = 1u;
    constexpr unsigned kAcceleration = 2u;
    }

"Relative" matters: h = 0 is the take-off reference, not the ground under
you. Terrain clearance will come from the LiDAR, separately.


C. WHAT COVARIANCE REPRESENTS
------------------------------
Next to the state, the filter keeps a 3 x 3 matrix P, the covariance:

    P = [ var(h)       cov(h,vz)    cov(h,az)  ]
        [ cov(vz,h)    var(vz)      cov(vz,az) ]
        [ cov(az,h)    cov(az,vz)   var(az)    ]

* The diagonal holds variances. Their square roots are the standard
  deviations ("sigma") that the output reports: altitudeSigma_m,
  velocitySigma_mps, accelSigma_mps2. Roughly 2 out of 3 errors should be
  smaller than 1 sigma, and 19 out of 20 smaller than 2 sigma, IF the
  filter's assumptions are right (section O shows when they are not).
* The off-diagonal terms say how errors are linked. Example: if the
  velocity estimate is too high, the altitude estimate (built from that
  velocity) is probably too high as well, so cov(h,vz) > 0. This link is
  why a barometer reading, which measures only h, can also correct vz.

A covariance must be symmetric and "positive definite" (every variance
positive, and no combination of errors with negative variance). The code
checks this with a Cholesky factorisation (section F).


D. WHY MEASUREMENT NOISE MATTERS
---------------------------------
Each measurement comes with a noise variance R. It is the filter's belief
about how wrong the sensor usually is. It decides how much the estimate
moves when a reading disagrees with it:

* R small: "the sensor is precise", so the estimate jumps toward the reading.
* R large: "the sensor is rough", so the estimate moves only a little.

Getting R wrong has consequences:

* R too small makes the filter nervous and over-confident. In the sweep,
  barometer sigma 0.25 m made it reject 79 good samples.
* R too large makes it sluggish.

Our values are in NavigationConfig.hpp:

    baroSigma_m     = 0.7f;    // voted barometric altitude, 1 sigma
    accelSigma_mps2 = 0.35f;   // vertical acceleration, 1 sigma

R = sigma^2, so R = 0.49 m^2 for the barometer and 0.1225 (m/s^2)^2 for the
accelerometer.


E. HOW A KALMAN FILTER WORKS
-----------------------------
A Kalman filter repeats two steps.

  1. PREDICT: move the state forward in time with a model, and let the
     covariance GROW, because the model is not perfect.
  2. UPDATE: when a measurement arrives, compare it with what the state
     predicts. The difference is the INNOVATION, nu = z - z_predicted.
     Move the state by a fraction of it: x = x + K * nu. The fraction K
     (the Kalman gain) is large when the state is uncertain and the
     sensor is good, and small the other way round. Then SHRINK the
     covariance, because we learned something.

One-line intuition with real numbers (they reappear in section H):

* The prediction says the altitude is 99.98 m with variance 0.25.
* The barometer says 99.60 m with variance 0.49.

The best blend weights each by the other's uncertainty:

    K = 0.25 / (0.25 + 0.49) = 0.338
    h = 99.98 + 0.338 * (99.60 - 99.98) = 99.85 m
    new variance = (1 - 0.338) * 0.25 = 0.165

The new variance is smaller than both 0.25 and 0.49: two imperfect
sources together beat either alone. Everything else is this idea, done
with matrices so that all three state variables move together.


F. HOW AN UNSCENTED KALMAN FILTER WORKS: SIGMA POINTS
------------------------------------------------------
The ordinary Kalman filter needs the model written as a matrix (linear).
A future paraglider model will not be linear: sink rate depends on airspeed
squared, bank angle, and so on.

The UNSCENTED Kalman filter handles nonlinear models without writing any
derivative. The trick:

  1. Pick 2n+1 = 7 carefully placed points around the current estimate,
     the SIGMA POINTS. Their spread matches the covariance exactly.
  2. Push EACH point through the real model, however nonlinear.
  3. Recompute a mean and covariance from where the 7 points landed.

How the points are placed (UnscentedKalmanFilter::makeSigma):

    P = L * L^T              (Cholesky: L is a "square root" of P)
    X0 = x
    X(i)   = x + sqrt(c) * column i of L     i = 1..3
    X(i+3) = x - sqrt(c) * column i of L
    c = alpha^2 * (n + kappa) = 1 * (3 + 0) = 3,   so sqrt(c) = 1.732

The weights:

    Wm0 = 0,  Wc0 = 2,  Wi = 1/6 for the other six

(alpha = 1, beta = 2, kappa = 0; design section 7 explains why. This beta is
NOT the Madgwick gain.)

Worked example (actual output of the code). Start with

    x = [100 m, -1 m/s, 0.5 m/s^2]
    P = diag(0.25, 0.04, 0.09)      (sigmas 0.5 m, 0.2 m/s, 0.3 m/s^2)

Then L = diag(0.5, 0.2, 0.3), and sqrt(c) * L = diag(0.866, 0.346, 0.520).
The 7 sigma points are:

    X0 = [100.000  -1.000   0.500 ]
    X1 = [100.866  -1.000   0.500 ]     X4 = [ 99.134  -1.000   0.500 ]
    X2 = [100.000  -0.654   0.500 ]     X5 = [100.000  -1.346   0.500 ]
    X3 = [100.000  -1.000   1.020 ]     X6 = [100.000  -1.000  -0.020 ]

Their weighted mean is exactly x, and their weighted spread is exactly P.
The test nav_sigma_points_reproduce_mean_and_covariance checks this.

If P is not positive definite, Cholesky fails, the filter cannot draw
points, and the code reports CovarianceNotPositiveDefinite instead of
continuing with garbage.


G. PROCESS-MODEL PROPAGATION (WORKED EXAMPLE)
----------------------------------------------
The process model says how the state moves in dt seconds. Today it is the
placeholder ConstantAccelerationModel (ConstantAccelerationModel.cpp):

    out[kAltitude]     = h + vz*dt + 0.5f*az*dt*dt;
    out[kVelocity]     = vz + az*dt;
    out[kAcceleration] = az;

That is school physics with constant acceleration over a short dt. Because
real acceleration does change, the model adds PROCESS NOISE Q(dt): "after
dt, I am this much less sure". Two parts:

  * qj = 3 m^2/s^5, white JERK: acceleration wanders randomly. Over 1 s
    without measurements the az sigma grows by sqrt(3) = 1.7 m/s^2.
  * qa = 0.02 m^2/s^3, unmodelled acceleration on h and vz: errors the az
    state cannot contain (accelerometer bias, attitude error, model error).
    Section O explains why this second term turned out to be essential.

For dt = 20 ms (one barometer period), the code computes

    Q(0.02) = [ 0.00000005  0.000004  0.000004 ]
              [ 0.000004    0.000408  0.000600 ]
              [ 0.000004    0.000600  0.060000 ]

  (e.g. Q[1][1] = qj*dt^3/3 + qa*dt = 3*0.000008/3 + 0.02*0.02 = 0.000408)

The engine (UnscentedKalmanFilter::predict) pushes all 7 sigma points
through propagate(), averages them and adds Q:

    x- = [ 99.980103  -0.990000  0.500000 ]

which is h = 100 - 1*0.02 + 0.5*0.5*0.0004 = 99.9801 and
vz = -1 + 0.5*0.02 = -0.99, as expected, and

    P- = [ 0.250017  0.000805  0.000023 ]
         [ 0.000805  0.040444  0.002400 ]
         [ 0.000023  0.002400  0.150000 ]

Notice two things:

* var(az) grew from 0.09 to 0.15. Most of that is Q's 0.06: the model
  is unsure about future acceleration.
* cov(h,vz) is no longer zero: a velocity error now implies an altitude
  error.

Because this model is linear, the UKF result must equal the textbook Kalman
filter's F*P*F^T + Q. The tests check that against an independent
double-precision filter (Tests/sim/LinearKalmanReference.hpp). For one
update they agree to within about 1e-6, and over 20 000 mixed steps to
within 1.5e-4 sigma.


H. MEASUREMENT CORRECTION AND THE KALMAN GAIN (WORKED EXAMPLE)
---------------------------------------------------------------
Every measurement here observes ONE state variable directly:

    barometer         z = h     (index 0)
    GNSS velocity     z = vz    (index 1)
    acceleration      z = az    (index 2)

In code this is a tiny lambda in VerticalNavigationEstimator::processCandidate:

    const auto h = [idx](const VerticalStateVec& x) {
        nav::Vec<1> z{};
        z[0] = x[idx];
        return z;
    };

The engine works in two halves on purpose:

  evaluate()  computes everything WITHOUT changing the state:
                z_hat = predicted measurement
                nu    = z - z_hat                        (innovation)
                S     = predicted spread of z + R       (innovation variance)
                Pxz   = how each state variable co-varies with z
                NIS   = nu^2 / S                         (surprise score)
  apply()     commits:  K = Pxz / S,  x = x + K*nu,  P = P - K*S*K^T

The split lets the estimator GATE: if NIS is absurdly large (a spike, a
fault), it can refuse the sample before anything changes.

Worked barometer update (continuing from G; z = 99.6 m, R = 0.7^2 = 0.49):

    z_hat = 99.980103      nu = -0.380104
    S     = 0.740019       NIS = 0.195       (well inside the 4-sigma gate: 16)
    K     = [0.337855  0.001088  0.000030]

    x+ = [ 99.851685  -0.990413  0.499988 ]
    var(h): 0.250 -> 0.166

The altitude moved a third of the way to the barometer (K = 0.338, as in
the one-line example in E). Velocity and acceleration barely moved: the
barometer is weakly linked to them so far.

Worked acceleration update (z = 0.8 m/s^2, R = 0.35^2 = 0.1225):

    nu = 0.300012     S = 0.272500
    K  = [0.000055  0.008807  0.550459]

    x+ = [ 99.851700  -0.987771  0.665132 ]
    var(az): 0.150 -> 0.067

Here K for az is 0.55: the filter was unsure about acceleration (variance
0.15) and the accelerometer is good, so az moves more than half way. vz
also moves slightly through cov(vz,az).

The covariance always SHRINKS in an update. That is why the model must
add Q in the predict step, or the filter would end up 100 % sure of
everything.


I. OUR THREE MEASUREMENTS IN PRACTICE
-------------------------------------
I.1 Barometer: each sample once, two barometers count once
  Preprocessing publishes a "voted" altitude (the mean of the two BMP585s
  if they agree) in EVERY 10 ms frame, but each barometer only measures
  every 20 ms, and the two are read in different cycles. If the filter used
  the vote every time its timestamp changed, it would use each barometer
  sample twice and believe it had more information than it does.
  BaroSampleTracker::classify() therefore accepts a vote only when EVERY
  contributing barometer has a sample newer than the one used last time:

    for each contributing barometer j:
        d = diffMs(channelTimestamp[j], lastUsed[j])
        if (d < 0) return OutOfOrder;
        if (d == 0) anyOld = true;
    return anyOld ? NotNew : New;

  Result: one observation every 20 ms, from two fresh samples. The test
  counts exactly 100 in 2 s, and 1 500 in 30 s through the real voter.
  If the two barometers disagree, preprocessing says Ambiguous and the
  filter skips the sample. It never guesses which one is right.

I.2 Vertical acceleration: from the attitude estimator, quality-aware
  The attitude estimator (Madgwick) already removed gravity:
  AttitudeEstimate::linearAccel.vertical_mps2. The navigation code never
  subtracts g again. It is used as a MEASUREMENT of az, never as a
  hidden input to the prediction, so it is used once, in one place.
  If the attitude is in doubt, the noise is inflated (classifyAccel):

    not reliable / Madgwick recovering ...... x3
    Madgwick turn gate active ............... x1.5
    no Madgwick accel correction for long ... x3
    IMU vote degraded ....................... x1.5
    total above 10, or a gyro gap ........... rejected

  These factors are reasonable guesses, NOT statistically calibrated. An
  invalid acceleration is never fed as 0: the sample is simply absent.

I.3 GNSS vertical velocity (optional)
  NAV-PVT reports velD, positive DOWN. The driver (NeoM9n.cpp line 68)
  already flips it:  verticalVelocity_mps = -velD * 0.001. So everything
  downstream is positive UP. The filter uses it only with a 3-D fix, at
  least 6 satellites, and a sensible speed accuracy (sAcc), with
  sigma = max(1.5 * sAcc, 0.15 m/s). GNSS ALTITUDE is not used: it is
  measured from sea level, while h is relative to the barometer reference.
  Mixing them would need an extra offset estimate.
  Without GNSS everything still works (the S21 test runs both ways).


J. TIME: TIMESTAMPS, DUPLICATES, LATE SAMPLES, WRAP-AROUND
-----------------------------------------------------------
The filter has its own clock, the "filter time" t_f: the moment the state
describes. For each new sample:

  sample newer than t_f    -> predict from t_f to the sample time, then update
  sample = previous one    -> NotNew, skipped (republished frame)
  sample older than the previous sample of the same sensor -> OutOfOrder, skipped
  sample after "now"       -> FutureTimestamp, skipped
  sample a little older than t_f (<= 30 ms; GNSS <= 300 ms)
                           -> applied, with R made LARGER by how much the
                              state can change in that gap (never treated
                              as current)
  older than that          -> TooLate, skipped

Within one frame, samples are sorted by time first, so a 50 Hz barometer
and a 100 Hz accelerometer interleave correctly.

Wrap-around: HAL_GetTick() wraps to 0 after 49.7 days. All comparisons use
the signed difference (int32_t)(a - b), which stays correct across the
wrap. The test runs the same flight once at normal times and once across
0xFFFFFFFF, and gets bit-identical results.

No new data for 50 ms: the filter predicts to "now" anyway, so its
uncertainty grows honestly during an outage.


K. STARTING, LOSING AND RECOVERING THE ESTIMATE
------------------------------------------------
States (NavState):

  Uninitialized -> no barometer yet (e.g. during the 5 s calibration)
  Initializing  -> collecting 0.5 s of barometer samples
  Tracking      -> normal
  Degraded      -> no barometer or no acceleration accepted for 0.5 s
  Recovering    -> barometer back after an outage; 10 good samples to confirm
  Unavailable   -> nothing for 3 s, no barometer for 20 s, or altitude
                   sigma > 50 m; outputs NaN; restarts when data return
  NumericalFault-> the engine refused a result; outputs NaN for that update,
                   keeps the fault code, restarts

Start-up in flight: the filter does NOT assume you are standing still.
tryFitAndStart() fits a straight line through 0.5 s of barometer samples:

* the line's height is the starting h;
* its slope is the starting vz;
* the fit's own standard errors become the starting uncertainty (never
  below 0.3 m and 0.3 m/s).

Simulation, starting at -3 m/s: valid after 0.52 s, velocity error +0.29
m/s with sigma 0.87 m/s.

Recovery from a persistent disagreement: if every barometer sample has
been rejected for 2 s (for example both barometers jumped by 15 m), the
filter concludes it is the one that is wrong. It inflates the altitude
variance by nu^2 and the velocity variance by (nu/T)^2, then accepts the
sample, so the estimate moves to it smoothly. This is counted and
reported. The trade-off: a fault that hits both barometers equally is
followed after 2 s.

Numerical faults: the engine checks every result (finite numbers, and a
covariance that passes Cholesky) BEFORE storing it. On failure it keeps the
old state and returns a reason. The estimator publishes NaN, raises
navflag::NumericalFault, records lastFaultCode, and restarts from the next
barometer window. Nothing is silently "fixed".


L. WHY THE CURRENT MODEL IS TEMPORARY, AND HOW TO REPLACE IT
-------------------------------------------------------------
Constant acceleration is not paraglider physics. A real wing has a sink
polar (sink rate vs airspeed), sinks faster when banked, and flies in
moving air. Nobody has built that model for ParaGuardian yet: the
repository was searched and has no polar, no coefficients and no data
(design section 3). The placeholder works now because the acceleration is
MEASURED 100 times a second. The model only has to bridge 10 ms gaps, and
it says so in every output (navflag::ModelPlaceholder).

The replacement boundary is IVerticalProcessModel.hpp:

    class IVerticalProcessModel {
    public:
        virtual ProcessModelInfo info() const = 0;
        virtual VerticalStateVec propagate(const VerticalStateVec& x, float dt_s,
                                           const ProcessModelInput& u) const = 0;
        virtual void processNoise(float dt_s, const VerticalStateVec& x,
                                  const ProcessModelInput& u, VerticalCov& Q) const = 0;
        virtual bool parametersValid() const = 0;
    protected:
        ~IVerticalProcessModel() = default;
    };

To replace the model:

  1. Write class MyWingModel final : public IVerticalProcessModel. propagate()
     may be as nonlinear as you like: the UKF never needs its derivative.
     Read bank angle etc. from ProcessModelInput u.
  2. In SensorProcessingTask.hpp, change the type of navModel_ (one line).
  3. Run the host tests and add tests for the new model.

The engine, the estimator, the sensors and every consumer stay unchanged.
This is shown by a test (nav_process_model_swap_with_nonlinear_mock_model)
that runs the same estimator with a deliberately different, nonlinear
"drag" model.

Honest limit: this interface has exactly 3 states. A model that needs
airspeed, wind or a barometer-bias state needs a bigger state. The ENGINE
already handles that (it is a template on N, and a test runs N = 4), but
the interface, the estimator's bookkeeping and the config would change.
Design section 6.3 lists exactly what.


M. HOW THE OUTPUT CONNECTS TO FUTURE SAFETY ALGORITHMS
-------------------------------------------------------
Every update produces a pg::NavigationEstimate (NavigationTypes.hpp), which
SensorProcessingTask publishes into a NavigationMailbox. A future flight-
state detector, landing guide or crash detector reads it like this:

    pg::NavigationEstimate e;
    if (SensorProcessingTask::Inst().navigation().read(e) && e.sequence != lastSeq) {
        lastSeq = e.sequence;
        if (e.velocityValid && e.verticalVelocity_mps + 2.0f * e.velocitySigma_mps < -6.0f) {
            // descending faster than 6 m/s even allowing for 2 sigma of error
        }
    }

The rules:

* Use a value only if its *Valid flag is set. Invalid values are NaN, never 0.
* Use the sigmas to decide how sure you are.
* Check state and qualityFlags.
* Never look inside the UKF or the model. NavigationEstimate::estimator and
  processModel say what produced the numbers, for logs only.

Because of this, swapping the model or falling back to the barometer-only
estimator (BaroAlphaBetaEstimator, which fills the same struct) needs no
change in the safety code.

For bring-up, HealthMonitorTask copies the key fields into g_systemHealth.
Watch it in STM32CubeIDE Live Expressions:

    navState, navAltitude_cm, navVerticalVelocity_mmps, navAltitudeSigma_cm,
    navBaroAccepted, navRecoveries, navNumericalFaults, ...


N. CODE TOUR: THE IMPORTANT CLASSES AND FUNCTIONS
--------------------------------------------------
NavMath.hpp
  Mat<R,C>, Vec<N>: fixed-size arrays, no heap. cholesky() returns false
  instead of producing NaN when a matrix is not positive definite.
  spdInverse() inverts S through Cholesky (for our 1 x 1 S it is just 1/S).

UnscentedKalmanFilter.hpp  (template on the state size N)
  configure(params)   computes the weights; refuses alpha <= 0 or n+kappa <= 0
  initialize(x, P)    refuses a P that fails Cholesky
  predict(model, dt, u)    sigma points -> model -> mean/cov + Q -> commit()
  evaluate(z, R, h, out)   innovation, S, Pxz, NIS; changes nothing
  apply(innovation)        x += K nu; P -= K S K^T; commit()
  commit()            the gatekeeper: finite? Cholesky OK? only then store
  Why "Y0 + sum W (Yi - Y0)" in weightedMean(): it avoids subtracting two
  large numbers at 1000 m altitude in float.

ConstantAccelerationModel.cpp
  propagate() and processNoise() as in section G. fromConfig() builds it
  from NavigationConfig so every caller uses the same noise.

BaroSampleTracker.hpp
  The "is this vote new?" rule of section I.1. It is shared by the UKF and
  the baseline, so both use every barometer sample exactly once.

VerticalNavigationEstimator.cpp
  update()             one call per frame: classify -> sort by time ->
                       initialise or process -> idle predict -> state ->
                       output
  classifyBaro/Accel/Gnss()   decide NEW? usable? which noise? (no maths)
  initializationStep() / tryFitAndStart()   the 0.5 s line fit of section K
  processCandidate()   predict to the sample time, late-sample inflation,
                       evaluate, gate, recovery, apply, bookkeeping
  updateState()        Tracking / Degraded / Recovering / Unavailable
  finalize()           fills NavigationEstimate; NaN when not tracking
  numericalFault()     report, clear, restart (counters survive)

NavigationInputAdapter.hpp
  navigationInputFrom(frame, attitude): copies fields, no maths. It is kept
  separate so the tests can drive the estimator with hand-made inputs.

BaroAlphaBetaEstimator.cpp
  The barometer-only comparison filter. Its gains are the steady-state
  Kalman gains for the actual dt (a test checks them against the Riccati
  equation).

SensorProcessingTask.cpp  (the only firmware change in the loop)
    navigation_.update(pg::navigationInputFrom(work_, attitude_.latest()));
    navigationOut_.publish(navigation_.latest());


O. WHAT THE SIMULATIONS SHOWED (ACTUAL VALUES) AND THE LIMITS
--------------------------------------------------------------
All of this is simulation with an independent "truth" generator
(Tests/sim/VerticalSimulator.hpp) that does NOT use the filter's model.
Full tables: VERTICAL_UKF_VALIDATION_REPORT.md.

Good:
  * Thermal-like lift cycles: velocity RMSE 0.129 m/s (UKF) vs 0.546
    (barometer-only).
  * Climb turning into descent: the UKF's velocity crosses the midpoint
    40 ms after the truth; the barometer-only filter takes 410 ms.
  * 30 minutes: uncertainty steady (sigma_h 0.130 -> 0.131 m), no
    numerical faults.
  * Duplicates, out-of-order frames and timer wrap-around: results
    identical to clean runs.
  * Real preprocessing + real Madgwick + UKF together: altitude within
    0.134 m, velocity within 0.053 m/s on a 1 m/s^2 sinusoidal motion.

Honest weaknesses:
  * A constant accelerometer bias of 0.2 m/s^2 biases the velocity by about
    0.27 m/s. Here the barometer-only filter is BETTER (0.222 vs 0.297 m/s
    RMSE). Fix later: calibrate the accelerometer, or add a bias state.
  * With realistic, time-correlated barometer errors, the altitude sigma
    is too optimistic. Only about 72 % of errors fall inside 2 sigma
    instead of 95 %.
  * A slow weather drift looks exactly like a slow climb (any relative
    barometer has this problem).
  * Execution time on the STM32 is estimated (50-150 us per update), not
    measured.

The lesson found during development: the first version (process noise on
jerk only) became very sure of its velocity because the accelerometer
reports 100 times a second. With an imperfect model or a biased
accelerometer, it ended 8 m/s wrong while claiming 4 cm/s of uncertainty,
and it threw away the barometer readings that disagreed. Adding qa (a small
"I might be wrong about acceleration in ways I can't see" noise on h and vz)
fixed that. The sweep confirms qa = 0 is the worst setting.


P. HOW TO EXPLAIN AND DEFEND THE DESIGN IN A CAPSTONE REVIEW
------------------------------------------------------------
Q: Why a UKF if the model is linear?
A: Because the next model will not be linear. The UKF engine is in place,
   verified now against an exact reference, and ready for that model,
   without any Jacobians to derive.

Q: How do you know the UKF is implemented correctly?
A: Because the model is linear, the UKF must equal a textbook Kalman
   filter. An independent double-precision filter with no shared code
   agrees to about 1e-6 per update and 1.5e-4 sigma over 20 000 steps. The
   only parameter set that disagrees (alpha = 0.001) does so for a
   documented float-precision reason, which the test demonstrates.

Q: Is the filter's uncertainty trustworthy?
A: When the sensors behave as assumed, yes, approximately (NEES 2.25 for
   an ideal of 3; 95.5-99.9 % of errors inside 2 sigma). With realistic
   correlated barometer errors, no: altitude sigma is optimistic. We
   report that rather than hide it.

Q: Why not just use the barometer?
A: We built that too (the baseline) and compared both on identical data.
   The UKF is better in 29 of 31 scenarios, mostly whenever the
   acceleration changes. It is worse with a large accelerometer bias, and
   we show it.

Q: What happens when a sensor fails?
A: One barometer: carry on with the other. Both disagree: the sample is
   skipped, never guessed. Both lost: carry on with the accelerometer,
   uncertainty grows, Degraded, then Unavailable after 20 s. Everything:
   Unavailable within 3 s, restart 0.5 s after data return. A numerical
   fault: NaN out, reported, restart. Safety code always knows, through
   state, flags and *Valid.

Q: Can you change the physics later?
A: Yes, behind one interface with a one-line change in the task. A test
   proves it with a different nonlinear model. If the new model needs
   more states, the engine already supports that, and the design lists
   exactly what else changes.

Q: What is NOT done?
A: No hardware or flight data, no measured timing on the STM32, no
   aerodynamic model, no tuning on real flights, no GNSS altitude, no
   LiDAR, and none of the safety logic that will use this output.

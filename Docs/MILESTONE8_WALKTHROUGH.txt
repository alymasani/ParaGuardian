PART I – FUNDAMENTALS
============================================================================

1. WHAT FLASH MEMORY IS
-----------------------
The W25Q128JV on the board is a 16 MiB "NOR flash" chip reached over SPI.
It keeps its contents with the power off (non-volatile), unlike the MCU's
RAM, which is lost at every reset. It is organised as
   65,536 pages  of 256 bytes   (the unit you PROGRAM)
    4,096 sectors of 4 KiB      (the smallest unit you ERASE)
Each cell survives about 100,000 erase cycles (datasheet minimum), so
software must spread erases ("wear levelling") and avoid needless writes.

2. ERASE VERSUS PROGRAM
-----------------------
Erasing sets every bit of a sector to 1 (bytes read 0xFF). It is slow:
typically 45 ms, at most 400 ms per sector.
Programming can only change bits from 1 to 0, and is fast (about 0.4 ms
per page, at most 3 ms). You cannot program a 0 back to 1; you must erase
the whole sector. Consequence: you cannot "overwrite" a record in place.
New data is APPENDED into still-erased space, and old sectors are erased
later as a whole. Also: a program must stay inside one 256-byte page; the
chip silently wraps to the start of the page otherwise (the M1 driver
rejects such writes).

3. WHY INTERRUPTED WRITES ARE DANGEROUS
---------------------------------------
Power can disappear at any moment (a crash damages the battery
connector!). If it disappears while a page is being programmed, some bits
of the new data reached 0 and some did not: the bytes are a random mix of
old and new. If it disappears during an erase, the sector is partly erased.
Software that reads such bytes later and believes them would restore a
nonsense emergency state, or worse, think there was no emergency.
The rule we follow: never trust a record unless it can PROVE it was
written completely, and never destroy the last good copy before the new
one is proven.

4. WHAT A JOURNAL IS
--------------------
A journal (or log-structured store) never modifies old data. Each change is
appended as a new, numbered record. The current state is simply "the
newest complete record". Our emergency journal uses two sectors as a ring
of 64-byte slots: when one sector is full, the OTHER sector (which holds
only older records) is erased and writing continues there.
Because the newest good record always lives in the sector being written,
erasing the other sector can never lose it. That is the whole power-loss
argument for rotation.

5. WHAT A CRC DOES (AND DOES NOT DO)
------------------------------------
A CRC-32 is a 32-bit checksum computed over the record. If any bit changes
by accident (torn write, worn cell, noise), the stored CRC almost certainly
no longer matches (undetected chance about 1 in 4 billion for random
damage). It is NOT security: anyone can compute a matching CRC for forged
data. Authentication needs a secret key (we use HMAC-SHA256 between the
phone and the server, see 11).
Our slots add a second guard: a "commit word" (0x3CC3A55A) written LAST,
after the body was read back and compared. A slot is valid only if magic,
version, CRC AND the exact commit word are present. A cut during the body
breaks the CRC; a cut during the commit word leaves some of its bits still
1, so it does not equal 0x3CC3A55A.

6. HOW RECOVERY WORKS
---------------------
At boot the storage task "mounts" the journal: it reads every slot of both
sectors (128 x 64 bytes) and classifies each one as erased, committed,
intact-but-uncommitted, or garbage. The committed slot with the highest
sequence number is the current state. Writing resumes after the last used
slot of that sector (skipping garbage). The emergency task then calls
EmergencyManager::restore(), which continues the incident under the same id
(table in the design document, section 6).
Flight logs mount similarly: the sector with the highest header sequence is
the newest; inside it, records are parsed page by page until the first
erased byte or the first record whose CRC fails.

7. WHY INCIDENT IDS MUST SURVIVE RESETS
---------------------------------------
The phone and the server recognise "the same emergency" by
(device id, incident id). That is what makes retries safe: the third copy
of incident 7 does not text your mother three times. If the MCU forgot its
counter at reset and started again at 1, a NEW crash could arrive as
"incident 1" and be thrown away as a duplicate of an old, finished
incident 1. That would silently swallow a real SOS.
Our rule: before an incident's request leaves the device for the first
time, the record saying "the next id is N+1" must be COMMITTED. Then no
later boot can reuse N. The wait is bounded (2 s): if the flash is broken,
the SOS goes out anyway with a flag "this id may repeat" so the phone and
server key it differently. Help is never delayed indefinitely for
bookkeeping.

8. WHY "QUEUED" DOES NOT MEAN "PERSISTED"
-----------------------------------------
The emergency task must never wait for flash (an erase can take 400 ms, and
an SOS countdown must keep ticking). So saving is split: save() copies 32
bytes into RAM and returns "Queued" plus a ticket number. The storage task
later writes it and announces "ticket 17 committed". Until then a power cut
loses it. The status therefore reports Pending, not Saved. Reporting a
queued write as saved would be a lie exactly when it matters (power loss).

9. HOW A STORAGE TASK OPERATES
------------------------------
StorageTask is a FreeRTOS task with LOW priority (20) that alone owns the
flash. It sleeps until notified (an emergency record was queued) or 50 ms
pass. Each step does ONE bounded piece of work in strict priority order:
emergency commit, critical log, telemetry log, maintenance. The emergency
record is checked before every step, so at most one step (worst case one
sector erase) delays it, no matter how much telemetry is waiting.

10. WHY LOGGING MUST NOT BLOCK SENSOR ACQUISITION
-------------------------------------------------
Acquisition reads the IMUs every 10 ms at priority 40. If it waited for a
400 ms erase, 40 samples would be lost and the crash detector and the UKF
would see a hole. So producers only copy a record (up to 48 bytes) into a
bounded queue and return; if the queue is full the record is dropped and
COUNTED. Telemetry and critical records use separate queues so a telemetry
burst cannot push out an emergency record.

11. HTTP AND TLS IN FIVE MINUTES
--------------------------------
HTTP is a request/response protocol: the phone sends "POST /v1/incidents"
with a JSON body; the server answers a status code (200 OK, 202 Accepted,
401 Unauthorized, 429 Too Many Requests, 503 Unavailable...) and a body.
TLS (the "S" in HTTPS) encrypts the connection and proves the server's
identity with a certificate signed by an authority the phone trusts, so
nobody on the network can read the location or impersonate the server.
TLS does not tell the SERVER who the phone is. For that every request is
signed: HMAC-SHA256(device secret, method + path + time + nonce +
SHA-256(body)). The server recomputes it; a wrong secret, an edited body,
a 5-minute-old timestamp or a nonce seen before are all rejected (replay
protection).

12. IDEMPOTENCY AND RETRIES
---------------------------
An operation is idempotent if doing it twice has the same effect as doing
it once. Networks lose answers: the phone sends the SOS, the server stores
it, the reply is lost, the phone retries. Without idempotency the contacts
get two texts; worse, retries during a long outage could spam them.
Server rules: an incident is keyed by (device, incident key); a repeated
revision changes nothing; each outbound message is a row that can exist
ONCE per (incident, contact, purpose). Retrying is therefore always safe
for the phone. On the provider side we cannot know whether a timed-out
request was sent, so such messages become "unknown" instead of being
re-sent automatically (at-most-once); a person can decide to resend.

13. WHY PROVIDER ACCEPTANCE IS NOT DELIVERY
-------------------------------------------
"HTTP 201 from Twilio" means: Twilio accepted the message into its queue.
It may still fail (unreachable phone, carrier filtering). "Delivered" comes
later through a status callback, and even that is the CARRIER saying the
handset received it, not that a human read it. The app and the device
therefore show separate stages: submission pending, server accepted,
provider accepted, delivered, failed, unknown.

14. ANDROID BACKGROUND-PROCESS LIMITATIONS
------------------------------------------
Android kills app processes to save memory and battery. Long work must run
in a FOREGROUND SERVICE with a visible notification (ours: type
"connectedDevice"). Since Android 12 an app may only START such a service
from the background in specific situations (e.g. after BOOT_COMPLETED for
this type, or when a companion device appears). After the user presses
"Force stop", Android delivers nothing to the app until the user opens it
again; no app can bypass that, and we do not claim to. We therefore keep
two separate facts: what the user REQUESTED (stored) and what is actually
RUNNING; the screen shows "interrupted, tap Resume" when they differ.

15. WHAT BATTERY STATE MONITORING MEANS
---------------------------------------
We can (once wired) measure the battery VOLTAGE through a divider and ADC,
and read the charger's "charging" and "done" pins. Voltage depends on
load, temperature and age; it supports "low" and "critical" warnings with
thresholds from the battery's own characterisation, NOT a precise
percentage. A percentage is only reported from a real fuel-gauge chip. We
add hysteresis (a level must recover 50 mV above its threshold) and require
3 consecutive samples, so a motor current spike does not flicker the
warning. Missing data is "Unknown", never "Healthy".

16. WHY THE 5 V REGULATOR AND AMPLIFIER CAN BE SOFTWARE CONTROLLED
------------------------------------------------------------------
Regulators and the MAX98357A have an enable pin: a GPIO can switch them.
Turning the amplifier off when silent saves current and avoids hiss; turning
the 5 V rail off saves the LiDAR's power. But switching costs time: a rail
needs milliseconds to settle and a device after it may need warm-up. So
PowerRail adds: a safe default at boot, a settle time before "ready", an
off-delay so it does not toggle between beeps, and fault detection from a
power-good signal if one exists.

17. WHY THE LIDAR MUST NOT BE SWITCHED OFF CARELESSLY
-----------------------------------------------------
The LiDAR measures distance to the ground. It feeds: flight-phase detection
(approach), landing warnings (low clearance, rapid closure), crash context
(was the ground close?) and missing-data alerts. If it is off, those
features silently degrade, and after power-up it needs warm-up time to be
trusted again. Default policy: keep it on. Only on Critical battery, on the
ground, with no open emergency, and only if explicitly configured, may it
be shed.

18. HOW AUDIO PATTERNS ARE SCHEDULED
------------------------------------
Three layers: the dispatcher picks ONE alert for the speaker by priority
(SOS countdown 100 ... advisory 20); the AudioAlertScheduler turns its
pattern ("3 beeps every 3 s") into timed tone requests; PcmToneOutput
turns each tone into 16-bit samples (square wave, 2 ms fade in/out against
clicks) for the I2S amplifier, filled from the DMA interrupt. A higher
priority alert pre-empts the current one (after a short fade); a tone is
only counted as audible if the amplifier was powered and ready.

19. WATCHDOGS AND TASK HEARTBEATS
---------------------------------
A hardware watchdog resets the MCU unless software "feeds" it regularly. It
recovers from a hang, but a blind feeder in a timer would keep feeding even
if the SOS task were stuck. So the health task feeds it only if every
critical task made PROGRESS (its counter moved) recently: that is a
heartbeat. A reset loses RAM, so while an incident's newest state is not
yet on flash we defer the reset up to 5 s for the storage task to commit
it. (The hardware watchdog is not enabled yet: bring-up section 11.)

20. HOW FAULT INJECTION WORKS
----------------------------
Instead of waiting for faults, tests cause them on purpose in simulated
parts. FakeNorFlash can lose power after exactly N programmed bytes, during
an erase, time out, fail reads, flip a bit, or vanish entirely. Because the
"reset" in the tests destroys every RAM object and keeps only the fake
flash bytes, each test is a faithful reboot. The journal test cuts power at
every one of the 65 byte positions of a commit; the persistence tests reset
during countdown, transmission, acceptance, delivery and cancellation.

PART III – WORKED EXAMPLES
============================================================================

EXAMPLE 1: crash -> countdown -> SOS -> persist -> reset -> recover -> BLE
           -> Android -> backend -> mock provider -> reconciliation
 t=0.0 s   CrashDetector reports CrashSuspected (episode 3).
           EmergencyManager::observeCrash -> openIncident: id = nextIncidentId
           = 4, next becomes 5; state Countdown(60 s); persist() -> store
           returns Queued, ticket 12. EmergencyAlertTask notices hasPending()
           and notifies StorageTask.
 t=0.002   StorageTask: commitEmergency -> journal slot 9 of sector A:
           body programmed, read back, commit word programmed, read back.
           reportCommitted(12). Next tick the manager sees committed >= 12:
           persist = Committed, committedNextId = 5 (id 4 is now durable).
 t=60.0    Countdown expires: createRequest (revision 1), persisted (ticket
           13). serviceTransport: identityDurable(4) is true -> submit to BLE.
 t=60.5    The battery connector bounces: power lost. RAM gone.
 boot      StorageTask mounts: newest committed slot says AwaitingPhoneAck,
           incident 4, revision 1, next id 5. EmergencyAlertTask waits for
           mounted(), restore(): same incident 4, revision 2, Restored flag,
           SosPending; boot record committed.
 +0.1 s    BLE sends SOS #4 rev 2 to the phone.
 phone     EmergencyEngine.receive: Key(device, 4) new -> stored; route
           INTERNET -> proceed(): stage SUBMISSION_PENDING, outbox row written
           (incidentKey "4", revision 2) BEFORE any network call.
           Worker: POST /v1/incidents, signed. Server: new incident, one job
           per consented contact, 202 {"overall":"pending"}. App:
           SERVER_ACCEPTED; reports PHONE_ACCEPTED + SEND_ATTEMPTED to MCU.
 server    Worker claims job (lease 60 s) -> FakeProvider accepted ->
           provider_accepted. (Real provider: same, through TwilioProvider.)
 +15 s     App polls GET /v1/incidents/4: provider_accepted -> stage
           PROVIDER_ACCEPTED (NOT delivered).
 later     Provider callback "delivered" (signature verified, de-duplicated)
           -> job delivered -> next poll: DELIVERED -> app reports
           DELIVERY_CONFIRMED -> MCU state Delivered, persisted.
 If the phone had retried the POST after a lost answer: "duplicate", no
 second job. If the MCU had re-sent revision 1: ignored as older.

EXAMPLE 2: normal flight -> sensor data -> logging -> flash corruption -> recovery
 Processing task every frame: FlightRecorder.logNavigation (Normal profile:
 once a second -> 37-byte record into the telemetry queue), logQuality
 (every 5 s), logCrash (only on state change). Emergency task logs every
 safety event (flight phase changes into the CRITICAL queue).
 StorageTask appends records into the page buffer of flight-log sector 812
 and programs the new bytes when the page fills or after 1 s.
 Power fails while page 7 of sector 812 is being programmed: the last
 record is torn. At boot: sector 812 has the highest header sequence; pages
 0-6 parse completely; page 7 ends at a record whose CRC fails -> the rest
 of page 7 is abandoned (tornAtMount 1), writing resumes on page 8 with the
 next sequence. On the ground: dump 0x020000.., run
   python3 Tools/pglog/pglog_decode.py flight.bin --region > flight.csv
 You get every complete record in order; the torn one is counted, not
 guessed. Had the flash chip died entirely, both logs and the journal mount
 Unavailable, every save is Rejected, the SOS path is unaffected and
 g_systemHealth shows the mounts as 1 (Unavailable).

EXAMPLE 3: low battery -> power policy -> amplifier -> critical alert
 Battery adapter (future) reports 3480 mV three times (thresholds configured
 for the real battery, say low 3500 / critical 3300): level Low. The
 emergency task (1 Hz) passes it to the dispatcher: LowBattery alert,
 Caution, priority 45 -> CautionDouble pattern. AudioAlertScheduler requests
 a beep; PcmToneOutput wantsAmplifier() -> audioRequested -> PowerManager
 turns on the 5 V rail (already on for the LiDAR), waits settleMs, enables
 the amplifier, waits its settle time -> audioReady -> setAmplifierReady(true)
 -> the DMA callback renders the 1100 Hz tone. 1.5 s after the last beep
 the amplifier is switched off again (hold time), the rail stays on (LiDAR).
 Later 3290 mV x3 -> Critical -> LowBattery escalates to Warning (priority
 70) and quiet alerts are restricted. A crash now opens an SOS countdown:
 priority 100 pre-empts everything, the emergency keeps the rail and the
 amplifier allowed, and the SOS request carries the battery millivolts so
 rescuers know the device may die soon.


============================================================================
PART IV – DEFENDING THE WORK
============================================================================
Questions you should expect, and the honest answers:

Q: "How do you KNOW the journal survives power loss?"
A: By construction (append-only, commit word last, newest copy never erased)
   and by test: power cut at every byte of a commit (65 positions), during
   erase, bit flips, total corruption. The limit: the fault model is ours;
   real silicon may fail in other ways (e.g. a partially programmed cell
   that reads differently later). The bring-up plan includes 1,000 real
   power cuts.

Q: "Why not save the state every 20 ms?"
A: Wear (100k cycles) and SPI time. State changes are events; we persist on
   every transition, which is what recovery needs.

Q: "What if the flash is broken during an emergency?"
A: The SOS is not delayed beyond 2 s; it is sent marked "id may repeat",
   and the status says persistence is degraded. Durability is reported, not
   assumed.

Q: "Can the server send the same SMS twice?"
A: Not by itself: jobs are unique, retries are idempotent, uncertain sends
   become "unknown". A duplicate can only happen if a person presses
   "retry unknown" for a message that had in fact gone out. Exactly-once is
   impossible over SMS; we chose at-most-once plus transparency.

Q: "Is HMAC with a stored secret secure?"
A: It authenticates devices and stops replays, over TLS. Its weakness: the
   server must store the secret (production: a secrets manager). It is a
   per-device credential, never a shared app secret, never in the APK.

Q: "Does monitoring survive everything on Android?"
A: No, and we do not claim it. Process death: sticky restart. Reboot: boot
   receiver. Device back in range: companion presence. Force stop: only the
   user can restart it; the app shows that clearly. None of the restart
   paths has been observed on a physical phone yet.

Q: "Why is the FIFO not enabled if it is better?"
A: Because its register map must be confirmed on the real part and the
   benefit measured with a drop rig. The decoding and draining are tested;
   switching acquisition to it without measurement would be a claim we
   cannot support.

Q: "What did testing find?"
A: Two real defects: (1) after the incident-id counter wraps, the identity
   hold could never end (fixed: no hold when ids are exhausted); (2) the
   GPIO LED backend never wrote the first "on" phase of a pattern (fixed).
   Several of our own wrong test expectations were also corrected, each
   explained in the validation report rather than silently changed.
